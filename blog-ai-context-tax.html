The Context Tax: Why Re-Explaining Things to AI Agents Costs Your Team

"So, like I said before..." may be the most expensive phrase in working with AI.

Oct 11, 2026 · 3 min read · by the Klober crew

"So, like I said before..." If you type some version of that into an AI chat every week, you're paying a quiet tax that never shows up on an invoice. It looks like a few lines at the top of every conversation: "I work at a small startup, we're building such-and-such, our customers are... our tone is..."

Call it the context tax. On a team, it adds up fast. This article won't give you a universal number, because there isn't one. Instead, it shows where the tax comes from and how to measure what your own team pays.

What Is Context, and Why Does It Matter So Much?

Context is everything the AI needs to give a good answer, not just a plausible one: who you are, what you're trying to achieve, what you've tried, which decisions have already been made, your style, your customer.

Without context, even the strongest model gives a generic answer. With it, the same model sounds like someone who works with you. The gap between those answers is often the gap between "nice" and "useful."

Where Does Context Get Lost?

Between conversations. You open a new chat, and it's all gone.

Between tools. You worked on something in one model, then moved to another. The context stayed behind.

Between people. One teammate explained the whole project to a bot. Another starts from scratch.

Over time. In long conversations, early details can carry less weight, and decisions from the start blur.

What the Tax Looks Like in Practice

Picture a small team where each person opens several AI chats a day and spends a few minutes briefing each important one: pasting documents and explaining what's happened so far. Sometimes a missed detail means an inaccurate answer that someone has to fix.

Each instance is trivial. The total isn't. Here's the formula: (people) × (important AI chats per day) × (minutes spent briefing each one) × (workdays per month). Plug in your own team's estimates.

How to Measure It on Your Team

For a better estimate, try a one-week experiment. Ask a few teammates to notice, without precise tracking, every time they explain something they've explained before, paste a background doc, or hunt for an old conversation. At the end of the week, compare notes.

The goal isn't an exact number but the pattern. Where is context lost most: between people, tools, or days? The answer tells you where to start and which fix will pay off most.

The Costs That Are Hard to Measure

Beyond time, there are costs that are hard to quantify:

  • Lower quality. When you're tired of explaining, you cut corners, and short briefs produce weaker answers.
  • Inconsistency. Everyone describes the project a bit differently, so the bots "understand" it differently.
  • Settled decisions get reopened. A bot that doesn't know an idea was rejected will suggest it again, and someone will waste time checking.
  • Declining usage. People who feel the AI "doesn't get them" simply use it less, and the team loses the value.

Four Ways to Cut the Tax Today

1. A background doc for each project. One page covering the project, goals, decisions, and tone. Update it when things change, and paste it at the start of important conversations.

2. Standing instructions. Many tools let you set system instructions or a profile. Invest in this once.

3. End-of-conversation summaries. Ask the AI to summarize what was decided, and save it somewhere shared.

4. A shared prompt library. Instead of everyone reinventing the briefing, build one good version everyone uses. (Our team prompting guide shows how.)

The Structural Fix: Shared Memory

These habits still depend on people remembering them. The structural fix shifts responsibility for context from people to the system: bots with persistent, shared memory that live in the channel where the team works and know what happened, even in conversations they weren't part of.

Then "like I said before" becomes unnecessary. The bot already has the background, and you can jump straight to the real question. Good habits shrink the tax; shared memory under your team's control can cut it dramatically.
